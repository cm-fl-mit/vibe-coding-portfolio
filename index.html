Starting Perspective: Constructionism + AI Skepticism

Where does AI sit as a creative partner - and a partner in creative, deep learning? As someone who has always believed in constructionist pedagogy - that we learn best by making things that matter to us - I was initially skeptical of AI coding tools. Wouldn't they shortcut the struggle that leads to understanding? Take away the satisfaction of coming up with creative solutions yourself? Replace the learning relationships between peers and students/mentors that are fundamental to contextual learning?

Working through my own demos and engaging with students doing thoughtful, creative, personal work in this course has helped structure my perspective on a more symbiotic relationship between constructionism and AI-supported construction.

While I'm still a "curious skeptic", I didn't see AI replacing human creativity or understanding in this course. Instead, if approached thoughtfully, these tools can open up new paths for exactly the kind of social, meaning-based learning that drives constructionism. They can help to clear tedious barriers while keeping (even amplifying) the joy of making THINGS - real artifacts that go beyond idea-space.

This shift echoes what Licklider envisioned in 1960: not machines replacing humans, but a "symbiosis" where computers handle "the routinizable work that must be done to prepare the way for insights." In our classroom, AI handled syntax and scaffolding while we humans focused on meaning, creativity, and connection.

The three projects I'll discuss here mark distinct stages in my evolving relationship with AI tools: from practical use, to experiencing how artifacts drive learning, to rediscovering creative identity.

Project 1: Laika Feeding Tracker

Making our lives easier + making the impractical possible

The Problem To Solve

My partner and I kept asking each other: "Did you feed Laika?" Our dog, opportunist that she is, would potentially eat duplicate meals (or not at all) depending on our miscommunication. I needed a simple shared tracker we could both check without constantly texting each other.

Could I have built this without AI? Technically, yes. Realistically? No - as evidenced by years of texting-based meal check-ins. I never found the project worth the time and energy it would have taken to write manually.

Collaborating with AI

Claude handled the boilerplate: state storage, phone-friendly app, responsive design. I could think about the functional and interaction details that actually worked for our daily routine and needs. Within an hour, I texted my partner a link and we had a working app on both our phones.

This was the first project in our class where I recognized vibe coding could produce something genuinely useful in my actual life. Before this, I'd been experimenting more superficially - making fun demos and explorations but not really pushing to make them "real" (getting them online, making them actually functional, caring if they worked tomorrow). I admittedly didn't dive deep into creative design in this project, but even my minimal creative investment still produced something functinoally valuable.

Licklider wrote about bringing computers into "the formulative parts of technical problems". Here, AI doesn't just solve pre-defined problems but helps us think through what we actually need. I went through several iterations of the most functional interactions for what we would need (did we actually want to get notified when the other person fed her? What about when we didn't feed her in time? Did we really need to track WHO fed her, or was getting fed the most important thing?). Working with AI didn't just write the code faster; it helped me rapidly iterate through different approaches until I found what worked for us.

A connection-maker

Several weeks later, some students asked me: "Are you still using the dog feeding tracker?"

This wasn't just politeness (I think!). They were curious about whether the thing I'd made had lasting value. Could we could make things that mattered in our real lives? A big element of constructionism is about learning through making things that are personally meaningful and worth sharing with others. Working with AI made it possible to actually make a useful tool within real-life time constraints.

Kate Crawford reminds us that AI is "embodied and material" - not abstract but built from "natural resources, fuel, human labor, infrastructures." Using AI to build something as mundane as a dog feeding tracker gave me a structure to reflect on this materiality. Every query has costs, but it also has benefits: enabling creation of something useful that wouldn't otherwise exist. This project made me think about other moments in life where I'm aware of the resources that are used based on my decisions: if I let food spoil because I didn't cook it in time, if I want to make a prototype that requires physical resources. This doesn't mean that I shouldn't eat food and shouldn't build experimental projects - but I should be intentional with my resource use. Even minimal engagement with AI tools can have the potential to produce something of real use in the world, but I want to be attentive to the actual needs I'm meeting and not wasteful.

Project 2: Wordle Duel

Games! + How artifacts create emotional investment

Beyond Tutorial Mimicry

In the past when I've worked with backend systems, I admittedly never had a strong grasp of what was happening. I would follow tutorials step by step, copying code and configurations without fully understanding the underlying logic. This time, partly because I was going to teach this material in a workshop and partly because I wanted it to actually work, I needed to build a better understanding.

Claude Code helped me build a multiplayer, competitive version of Wordle, with "rooms" where friends could play together. But the way Claude initially set up the site created a security issue - the room system was incompatible with Firebase's default security settings, and either players could potentially access data they shouldn't, or wouldn't have privileges to create new rooms.

This problem forced me to dig deeper than I had before. I went through the Firebase documentation, understanding why most API keys aren't safe to share publicly, and learning how Firebase handles this through read/write rules. I finally understood the relationship between client-side code, server-side security, and database access patterns. These were concepts I'd encountered before but never truly grasped because I'd never had to fix something that mattered to me (in truth, I had avoided building full-stack systems independently).

Artifacts as Catalysts for Learning

The key discovery here was about the role of AI in creating real emotional investment. Having a tangible game - something I could actually play, something that almost worked, that I was itching to share with friends - created far more motivation to understand the underlying systems than any tutorial or abstract concept ever had. The artifact itself became the driver for deep learning.

I saw this pattern repeatedly with students. Many of them started encountering web architecture — frontend, backend, servers, databases — without ever hearing those terms explicitly. They built sites with Claude Artifacts or Replit, then had problems: their site wouldn't work when they sent it to friends, or it worked locally but not when deployed online, or they were being charged a lot(!) of money to host a server on custom platforms.

By that point, they had already created something they were proud of and wanted to share. This investment acted as their constructionist catalyst. Understanding client-server architecture wasn't an abstract exercise anymore— it was the barrier between them and showing their work to others. Students who might have glossed over slides on web architecture were suddenly engaged in understanding why their code wasn't working.

AI Meets ZPD

Something I've been especially critical of with AI-supported learning is the loss of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), the teaching and learning relationships that form between students at slightly different levels. Here, I saw this emerging within the context of these projects: students at different levels of systems understanding began supporting each other. Someone who understood file paths helped someone struggling with deployment. Someone who grasped APIs explained them to someone hitting authentication errors. The AI had created artifacts at just the right level of "almost working" to create these zones of proximal development where peer learning could emerge naturally. Sometimes, the brokenness and bugginess of the AI system (even the shared experience of "why is this telling me it's working, when it's not!") actually acted as a catalyst for sharing learning experiences.

(Thanks to Ben Wheeler for helping me think through these observations during helpdesk hours - how the students' emotional investment in their projects drove technical learning in ways that traditional instruction rarely achieves.)

Prerequisites Still Matter

This kind of learning did require some baseline knowledge. I knew enough about web development to prompt Claude for specific debug information, and had some sense of where to start investigating. A complete beginner might have simply been stuck, unable to bridge the gap between the AI's output and the specific problems they were encountering.

Dreyfus argued that human intelligence depends on embodied experience and situational understanding that can't be fully formalized. The AI could generate correct code, but understanding why it wasn't working in this specific context — with these particular security requirements, in this hosting environment — required human experience and judgment. The AI was most valuable not as a replacement for understanding but as a tool that allowed me to work with systems complex enough to be worth understanding.

Project 3: Breathing Machine

Making magic + Redeveloping creative identity

Making the Invisible Visible

I'm a long-time believer in the power of physical and kinetic systems to feel magical - and I've been curious to explore the interplay between vibe coding and electronics. For this project, and in honor of the AI theme of the class, I wanted to give physical form to something invisible: computational effort. Using a small motor and Arduino, I created a system that "breathes" faster when the computer works harder. CPU load translated into mechanical rhythm.

This type of simple, expressive, playful project is a throwback to the type of creative work that led me to learn to code in the first place, as an undergrad. This creative expression - simple ideas and interactions expressed through code and physical systems - was a big part of my identity as an artist, a community organizer in art, tech, and learning spaces, and an educator. However, as other time-consuming components of life have ramped up, my productivity in creative outlets has gone down. When this happens, it's easy to feel that creativity (and self-identity as a creative person) is idea-limited. Working with AI tools has instead given me the confidence that my creativity is actually more time-limited - a much kinder problem to solve.

AI as Creative Assistant

AI assistance made this project possible in an evening instead of a weekend. The code for reading CPU load, working with a web Serial interface, creating the breathing rhythm - this was built in minutes, prioritizing my time for creative play: tuning the rhythm and interaction until it felt right, building the physical housing, treating this like an assistant helping to build my original idea.

I've worked in the past as a hired contractor building physical computing systems for famous artists to display in galleries - as their own work, based on their design spec. In this project, it felt like AI was playing that role for me as my assistant, catalyzing my ability to actually turn ideas into reality in a more (time and energy) sustainable way.

The Authenticity Question

Of course, the question persists: did I really "make" it if AI wrote much of the code?

This question is still valid, and I think it should be. Crawford's critique resonates: we need to understand AI as a system of power, extraction, and labor. When I use AI to code, whose labor am I drawing on? What data was extracted to train these models? Maybe that of the communities of people I worked alongside in the earlier days of media art?

Wiener's framework offers another lens. He distinguished between the first industrial revolution (replacing human power) and the second (replacing human judgment). If we're careful, we might be able to build a new framework for this new relationship: not replacement but augmentation of human creativity. The physical form, the conceptual connection between computation and breath, the idea to make a physical object that feels "magical": these emerged from my imagination. The AI handled the implementation details, like a skilled assistant might help an artist realize their vision.

AI as Creative Partner

I think there's still a lot of discovery and nuance ahead in determining what feels like "authentic" making with AI. When the AI suggests something clever I wouldn't have thought of, is that collaboration or substitution? Should I resist it just because it didn't come from me, or should I accept it as a productive building-upon, like I would with a human thought partner? When it handles boilerplate, that clearly feels like assistance. When it generates creative solutions, things get murky. An enormous part of the creative process is sitting with uncertainty, feeling the frustration of not having the right idea, and learning to iterate and reflect - this leads to developing creativity as a practiced skill, not an innate talent. What happens to this skill-building if there's temptation (and opportunity) to hit a creative wall and immediately query ChatGPT: "Give me 5 related ideas"?

However, scaffolding creative skills also involves building the ability to form one's language, one's judgments and opinions, and engage in critical reflection. As Dreyfus noted, the most sophisticated human activities (what he called "nonformal behavior") depend on "meaning and situation which are not explicit." The meaning I make from these projects, the situations they address, the connections and insight they create - these remain fundamentally human, even when the implementation is assisted.

I believe that AI can be a partner in creative learning when we design for it. All the social engagement I witnessed — students helping each other debug, sharing discoveries, being motivated to get working versions online to share with each other, building on each other's work — happened in a class specifically structured for collaboration. The AI tools lowered barriers to creation, but the classroom provided the audience, the feedback loops, the reason to share. (Some students went above and beyond, motivated to make things that their friends beyond the class could also explore or play!)

We need not just better code assistants, but AI ecosystems that actively support the social dimensions of learning. Tools that make sharing natural, that connect learners facing similar challenges, that celebrate iteration over perfection. In education, this also means using AI not to avoid the hard parts of learning, but to make it possible to build more ambitious, meaningful projects that connect to students' actual lives and to each other.
